Sensation

  • Sensation is the conscious or subconscious awareness of changes in the external or internal environment.
  • Perception is the conscious awareness and interpretation of sensations and is primarily a function of the cerebral cortex.
  • The nature of a sensation and the type of reaction generated vary according to the destination of sensory impulses in the CNS.
  • Each different type of sensation is a sensory modality; usually, a given sensory neuron serves only one modality.
  • General senses include somatic senses (touch, pressure, vibration, warmth, cold, pain, itch, tickle, and proprioception) and visceral senses; special senses include the modalities of smell, taste, vision, hearing, and equilibrium.
  • For a sensation to arise, four events typically occur: stimulation, transduction, generation of impulses, and integration.

Somatic Sensory Pathways

  • Somatic sensory pathways from receptors to the cerebral cortex involve first-order, second-order, and third-order neurons.
  • Adaptation is a decrease in sensitivity during a long-lasting stimulus.
  • Receptors are either rapidly adapting or slowly adapting.
  • Axon collaterals (branches) of somatic sensory neurons simultaneously carry signals into the cerebellum and the reticular formation of the brainstem.

Somatic Sensations

  • Nerve impulses for touch, pressure, vibration, and proprioception ascend to the cerebral cortex along the posterior column–medial lemniscus pathway.
  • Somatic sensations include tactile sensations (touch, pressure, vibration, itch, and tickle), thermal sensations (warmth and cold), pain, and proprioception.
  • Receptors for tactile, thermal, and pain sensations are located in the skin, subcutaneous tissue, and mucous membranes of the mouth, vagina, and anus.
  • Receptors for touch are (a) tactile corpuscles of touch and hair root plexuses, which are rapidly adapting, and (b) slowly adapting
  • Nerve impulses for pain, temperature, touch, and pressure ascend to the cerebral cortex along the anterolateral pathway.
  • Nerve impulses for pain, temperature, touch, and proprioception from the face, nasal cavity, oral cavity, and teeth ascend to the cerebral cortex along the trigeminothalamic pathway.

Control of Body Movement

  • All excitatory and inhibitory signals that control movement converge on motor neurons, also known as lower motor neurons or the final common pathway.
  • Neurons in four neural circuits participate in control of movement by providing input to lower motor neurons: local circuit neurons, upper motor neurons, basal nuclei neurons, and cerebellar neurons.
  • Neurons of the corpus striatum assist movement by providing input to the upper motor neurons.
  • They help initiate and suppress movements.
  • Vestibular nuclei in the medulla and pons play an important role in regulating posture; the reticular formation helps control posture and muscle tone; the superior colliculus allows the body to respond to sudden visual stimuli and permits rapid movements of the eyes; and the red nucleus permits fine, precise, voluntary movements of the distal parts of the upper limbs.
  • The cerebellum is active in learning and performing rapid, coordinated, highly skilled movements.

Integrative Functions of the Cerebrum

  • The primary motor cortex (precentral gyrus) is a major control region for executing voluntary movements.
  • Sleep and wakefulness are integrative functions that are controlled by the suprachiasmatic nucleus and the reticular activating system.
  • The axons of upper motor neurons extend from the brain to lower motor neurons via direct and indirect motor pathways.
  • Non-rapid eye movement sleep consists of four stages.
  • The direct pathways include the corticospinal pathways and the corticobulbar pathway.
  • The corticospinal pathways convey nerve impulses from the motor cortex to skeletal muscles in the limbs and trunk.

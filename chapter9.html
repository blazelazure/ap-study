  • joint classifications, movements, and synovial joint structure

Core Concepts

  • The synovial membrane secretes synovial fluid, which forms a thin, viscous film over the articular cartilages and other surfaces within the articular capsule.
  • Many synovial joints also contain accessory ligaments (extracapsular and intracapsular) and articular discs or menisci.
  • A labrum is a fibrous cartilage lip that extends from the edge of the joint socket and helps deepen the socket.
  • Synovial joints contain an extensive nerve and blood supply.
  • The nerves convey information about pain, joint movements, and the degree of stretch at a joint.
  • Blood vessels penetrate the articular capsule and ligaments.

Types of Movements at Synovial Joints

  • In a gliding movement, the nearly flat surfaces of bones move back and forth and side to side.
  • In angular movements, a change in the angle between bones occurs.
  • Examples are flexion–extension, lateral flexion, and abduction– adduction.
  • Circumduction refers to the movement of the distal end of a body part in a circle and involves a continuous sequence of flexion, abduction, extension, adduction, and rotation of the joint (or in the opposite direction).
  • In rotation, a bone moves around its own longitudinal axis.
  • Special movements occur at specific synovial joints.

Factors Affecting Contact and Range of Motion at

  • The ways that articular surfaces of synovial joints contact one another determine the type of movement that is possible.
  • Factors that contribute to keeping the surfaces in contact and affect range of motion are structure or shape of the articulating bones, strength and tension of the joint ligaments, arrangement and tension of the muscles, apposition of soft parts, hormones, and amount of use.

Selected Joints of the Body

  • A summary of several selected joints of the body, including articular components, structural classification, and movements, is presented in Tables 9.3 and 9.4.
  • The temporomandibular joint (TMJ), shoulder joint, elbow joint, hip joint, and knee joint are described in Sections 9.10 through 9.14.

Temporomandibular Joint

  • The temporomandibular joint (TMJ) is between the condyle of the mandible and mandibular fossa and articular tubercle of the temporal bone.
  • The temporomandibular joint is a combined hinge and plane joint.

Glenohumeral (shoulder) Joint

  • The glenohumeral joint is between the head of the humerus and glenoid cavity of the scapula.
  • It is a type of ball-and-socket joint.

Elbow Joint

  • The elbow joint is between the trochlea of the humerus, the trochlear notch of the ulna, and the head of the radius.
  • The elbow joint is a type of hinge joint.

Hip Joint

  • Types of synovial joints are plane, hinge, pivot, ellipsoid, saddle, and ball-and-socket.
  • The hip joint is between the head of the femur and acetabulum of the hip bone.
  • In a plane joint the articulating surfaces are flat, and the bones primarily glide back and forth and side to side (many are biaxial); they may also permit rotation (triaxial); examples are joints between carpals and tarsals.
  • The hip joint is a type of ball-and-socket joint.
  • In a hinge joint, the convex surface of one bone fits into the concave surface of another, and the motion is angular around one axis (uniaxial); examples are the elbow and ankle joints.

Knee Joint

  • The knee joint is between the patella and patellar surface of the femur; the lateral condyle of the femur, the lateral meniscus, and the lateral condyle of the tibia; and the medial condyle of the femur, the medial meniscus, and the medial condyle of the tibia.
  • In a pivot joint, a round or pointed surface of one bone fits into a ring formed by another bone and a ligament, and movement is rotational (uniaxial); examples are the atlanto-axial and radioulnar joints.
  • The knee joint is a modified hinge joint.
  • In an ellipsoid joint, an oval projection of one bone fits into an oval cavity of another, and motion is angular around two axes (biaxial); examples include the wrist joint and metacarpophalangeal joints of the second through fifth digits.
  • With aging, a decrease in synovial fluid, thinning of articular cartilage, and decreased flexibility of ligaments occur.
  • In a saddle joint, the articular surface of one bone is shaped like a saddle and the other bone fits into the saddle like a sitting rider; movement is biaxial.

Aging and Joints

  • Most individuals experience some degeneration in the knees, elbows, hips, and shoulders due to the aging process.

Arthroplasty

  • Arthroplasty refers to the surgical replacement of joints.
  • The most commonly replaced joints are the hips, knees, and shoulders.
